# 1. Introduction

Single-person households increasingly live in small studio apartments in which cooking, laundry drying, studying and sleeping share one room of less than 20 m². In such a room, the goals of indoor environmental control are tightly coupled. Turning on the range hood during cooking depressurizes the room and draws outdoor fine particulate matter through the envelope [@abdullahi2013emissions]; running a dehumidifier to dry laundry releases its condenser heat into the same small volume; humidifying dry winter air raises the dew point above the temperature of a cold window pane [@alduchov1996improved]; and the ventilation required to keep carbon dioxide at a level that does not impair cognitive performance [@satish2012co; @allen2015associations] removes the heat that a boiler has just supplied. An action that is correct for one device can therefore be wrong for the room as a whole, in summer and in winter alike.

Residential occupants typically handle these situations with the built-in automation of each appliance and, increasingly, with smart-home hubs that let users connect devices through trigger–action rules [@ur2014practical]. Low-cost sensors now make it practical to monitor temperature, humidity and particulate matter in real time [@dileo2025design], and smart thermostats add schedule-based setback [@stopps2021residential]. However, rules written device by device do not know about each other: users misinterpret rule behavior [@brackenbury2019how], and conflicting or unsafe rule combinations must be detected after the fact [@fu2020conflict; @sun2024threat]. None of these mechanisms decides which of several available devices should act when two goals compete.

Building control research has addressed the coupling between comfort, air quality and energy mainly through model predictive control (MPC) and reinforcement learning (RL) [@drgoa2020all; @wang2020reinforcement; @zhang2022building; @halhoulmerabet2021intelligent; @almindeel2024energy]. Comparative studies show that the relative merit of MPC and RL depends strongly on the available data, model fidelity and test conditions [@arroyo2022comparison; @wang2023comparison; @kumkam2025comparative; @mulayim2025comparative], and learning-based occupancy-aware controllers usually assume months of data from a single heating, ventilation and air-conditioning (HVAC) system [@esrafiliannajafabadi2021occupancybased; @hou2025occupancydriven]. Multi-objective studies typically control one HVAC actuator in offices or test cells [@mei2017energyefficient], and window operation is studied mostly as occupant behavior rather than as a controlled actuator [@sorgato2016effect]. Benchmark environments such as BOPTEST [@blum2021building] and Sinergym [@jimnezraboso2021sinergym] have improved reproducibility, but they model buildings with a single conditioning plant and cannot represent a room in which an air conditioner, a dehumidifier, a humidifier, a purifier, a range hood, fans, a motorized window and a boiler all act on the same air volume.

Three gaps follow. First, the coordination of many heterogeneous, low-cost actuators — household appliances that the occupant already owns, combined with inexpensive retrofit devices — to resolve conflicting indoor goals has received little attention compared with the optimization of a single HVAC plant. Second, controllers proposed for such settings are rarely compared, under one evaluation protocol, against both the commercial-grade rule automation that occupants actually use and learning-based alternatives; evaluation designs are seldom fixed before the results are seen [@nosek2018preregistration], which leaves room for favorable choices of scenarios, criteria and baselines. Third, actuating a window in an occupied room raises a pinch-safety requirement that is usually met with additional sensors, whereas sensorless detection from the motor current is established for automotive windows [@cheng2021research; @vidlak2021sensorless] but has not been integrated into building control.

This study addresses these gaps with a simulation of a 3.5 m × 5 m studio apartment built in NVIDIA Isaac Sim and ROS 2 [@nvidia_isaacsim; @macenski2022robot]. Seven low-cost sensors and three non-sensor signals feed a cooperative controller that drives eleven actuators and, in a heating-season extension, a combi gas boiler with hydronic floor heating. The contributions are as follows. (1) A cooperative control structure in which layered arbitration and cost-ordered "means ladders" assign each goal to the cheapest admissible actuator and pass it to the next one when a constraint blocks it. (2) A sensorless window actuator that estimates position from the commutation ripple of the motor current and limits the pinch force from the motor effort. (3) An evaluation protocol in which commercial-grade baselines are specified before any result is seen, held-out scenarios are pre-registered and run once, pass/fail criteria are restricted to environmental outcomes, and effects are reported with scenario-cluster bootstrap intervals [@field2007bootstrapping] and Holm-corrected tests [@holm1979simple]. (4) A two-axis comparison: the cooperative controller is compared with device-independent automation, commercial-grade rule automation with and without continuous ventilation, and an ablation without hysteresis at the system level, and the energy-critical decision of away-time cooling is compared with long short-term memory (LSTM) and RL alternatives trained on the same data.

Section 4 presents the control method and the sensorless window. Section 5 describes the evaluation protocol.
